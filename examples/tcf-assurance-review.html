Assurance could not be given, because what the system does cannot be traced to what the law says

Not a technical failure. A framework whose rules were never documented against the legislation that authorises them.

1The question asked

Why the review exists and what it was allowed to conclude.

Commissioned in December 2024 to assure the Secretary on how the framework actually operates (§1.1).
Three questions: lawful compliance decisions, a reliable IT system, sufficient governance (§1.1).
The framework has run since 1 July 2018, deciding compliance automatically at scale (§1.1).
Advisory work, not an audit: the report states that no assurance-standard opinion is expressed.
2How it was done

The methods that produced the evidence.

Document analysis, semi-structured interviews and detailed process maps (§1.2).
A separate technical workstream examined the code base and system architecture (§1.2).
A literature review and comparison against international compliance models (§1.2).
Working hypotheses were refined as evidence arrived, rather than fixed at the start.
3What it found

The assurance outcome, in the report’s own terms.

Three scope items assessed Not effective: delivery, IT system, governance (§2, Table 1).
Insufficient evidence that the framework aligns with its authorising legislation (§1.3.1).
Over 370 business rules in the system, not consistently tied to law or policy (§1.3.1).
No records of the discretionary decisions the statute requires a delegate to make (§1.3.1).
Assurance rests on traceability. Traceability was the thing that was missing.
4Why it happened

Where the report locates the origin of the problem.

Most issues stem from the 2018 design and build, not from later operation (§1.3).
Legislative intent became system logic without a documented program design (§1.3.1).
Incremental changes accumulated with no mapping back to program logic (§1.3.1).
Two misconstructions stayed partly concealed for more than five years (§1.3.1).
The report rejects the word “defect”: these are translation failures, not bugs.
5The eight themes

The spine everything else hangs from.

Non-technical: traceability, automation versus individualisation, three lines of defence, iterative policy change (§3.1).
Technical: architecture degradation, design and implementation, system roadmap, release controls and testing (§3.1).
Every recommendation answers one of the eight themes (§4.1).
Eight themes carry 237 pages. This is the outline the report was written from.
6What it asks for

The first theme’s recommendations, with the timeframes attached.

A traceability register linking legislation and policy to business rules and system logic (1NT.1, 3–6 months).
Controls that let a lawful decision override or defer an automated action (1NT.2, three months or less).
Participant pathway and workflow mapping across the IT system (1NT.4, 12–18 months).
One version-controlled repository replacing fragmented documentation (1NT.5, 3–6 months).
